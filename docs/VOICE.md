# The house voice

Every recipe in the library reads like it came from one cook: half confident
nonna, half tired friend who's very good at this. Bossy because they care,
honest because they've made every mistake already.

## The shape of a recipe

0. **Title** (frontmatter `title:`). A house title, never the source's. See below.
1. **Blurb** (frontmatter `blurb:`). One or two short sentences. What it is, then a
   wink. Under 30 words.
2. **## Before we start.** A short note, 2 to 4 sentences: the one thing that makes
   this dish work, or the mistake to avoid, said like a friend leaning on the counter.
3. **## Ingredients.** Untouched. Quantities, names and groupings stay exactly as
   transcribed — the shopping list is built from them.
4. **## Instructions.** Same steps, same order, same numbers (times, temperatures,
   amounts). Plain, clear instruction first; a quip after it on maybe a third of
   the steps, never all of them. A step must still work if you skip the joke.
5. **## Kitchen's closed.** One line to finish: a storage tip, a cleanup truth, a
   leftovers plan, or a serving move. Kitchen-y and useful, with a smile on it.

## How it sounds

- Short sentences. Second person. Contractions.
- Confident commands: "Salt it like it owes you money." "Off. The heat."
- Confessions: "I have served the clumpy version and called it rustic."
- Reassurance at the scary step: "It will hiss at you. That's normal."
- **Bold** only for the one warning that ruins the dish if ignored, at most one
  per recipe.

## Titles

The title is ours too. Sources name dishes like a menu; we name them like a friend
telling you what's for dinner.

- Short: about 6 words at most.
- The dish stays recognizable. A classic keeps its name and gets a nudge:
  "Cacio e Pepe e Patience", "Lasagne Bolognese, Layer by Layer".
- Mix the shapes so the list doesn't rhyme with itself: a pun ("Rigatoni Takes a
  Leek"), a little scene ("Jerk Chicken Tacos on Island Time"), a verdict
  ("Smash Burgers, No Apologies"), a twist on a phrase ("Skin in the Game").
- A variation names what's different: "Passed-Out Cacio e Pepe", not a second
  "Cacio e Pepe".
- No wordplay on accents, languages, or how anyone talks. The joke is on the dish.
- New recipes get their file name and URL from the house title. The first 40 kept
  their original file names so their links didn't change.

## Running bits (use, don't overuse — any one bit in only a few recipes)

- Salt the water like it owes you money.
- The failed version you "meant to make rustic."
- Future you / your future self, who will be grateful.
- The cook's portion: the crispy bits eaten standing at the stove.
- "Ask me how I know."
- Don't wander off / stay close.
- Things that hiss, spit or complain are "normal; they do that to everyone."

## Rules

- Never change a quantity, time, temperature or ingredient. The voice goes around the
  facts, not into them.
- No jokes at anyone's expense except the cook's (and mildly, the dish's).
- Keep the source link. The words are ours; the credit for the idea is theirs.
- No food-safety jokes that could be read as advice (undercooking, leaving food out).
